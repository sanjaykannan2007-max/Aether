Concept: Quantum Tunneling Explained

In classical physics, a particle cannot overcome a barrier if its energy (E) is less than the barrier's potential (V₀). However, due to wave-particle duality, quantum particles possess a continuous wave function (ψ). When this wave hits a barrier, it doesn't drop to zero instantly but decays exponentially. If the barrier is thin enough, a non-zero amplitude emerges on the other side, creating a probabilistic chance of the particle "tunneling" right through solid matter.

Concept: Wave-Particle Duality

When unobserved, electrons or photons drift through both slits simultaneously as probability waves, interfering with themselves to create a fringe pattern. However, placing a detector (Detector ON) at the slits forces the wave function to collapse into a single definitive path. Because the 'which-path' information is now known, the particles act like classical bullets, destroying the interference pattern completely. This is the core of the quantum measurement problem.

Concept: Infinite Potential Well

A particle confined to an infinitely deep, impenetrable box can only possess specific, quantized energy levels (denoted by the quantum number n). Because the matter-wave boundary conditions must equal zero exactly at the walls (the particle physically cannot exist outside), only perfect standing waves can form. Consequently, a quantum particle can never have exactly zero energy, securing a mandatory baseline of activity known as "zero-point energy".

Concept: Hydrogen Orbitals

Unlike the classical Bohr model showing neat planetary rings, electrons actually exist as fuzzy, 3D probability clouds spanning around the nucleus known as orbitals. The shape and density of these clouds are defined strictly by quantum numbers: principal (n), azimuthal (l), and magnetic (m). S-orbitals (l=0) are perfectly spherical grids, while P-orbitals and D-orbitals form elegant lobe and donut geometries shaped by spherical harmonics.
